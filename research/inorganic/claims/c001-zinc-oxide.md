### 1.2 Crystal structure (C1)

ZnO crystallises in three structures:
- **Wurtzite** (hexagonal P6₃mc, a = 0.3296 nm, c = 0.5207 nm) — thermodynamically stable at ambient T/P; the form used in cosmetics. (C1)
- **Zinc-blende** (cubic) — only on cubic substrates.
- **Rocksalt** (B1, NaCl structure) — only at high pressure (>10 GPa).

The Zn²⁺ is tetrahedrally coordinated with O²⁻ in the wurtzite lattice, giving rise to a strong polar c-axis. *Source:* Özgür et al., *J. Appl. Phys.* 98, 041301 (2005); SCCS/1489/12 §3.1.

### 1.3 Bandgap & cutoff wavelength (C1 / C3)

- **Bulk wurtzite ZnO direct bandgap, 300 K: E_g = 3.37 eV.** (C1; multiple sources — Özgür et al. 2005; Schneider & Lim 2019; Klingshirn 2007 *Phys. Stat. Sol. B* 244:3027.)
- Some authors cite 3.20–3.30 eV for nanoparticles depending on size / quantum confinement (Smijs & Pavel 2011 cite 3.22 eV at 77 K for wurtzite, 3.32 eV zinc-blende).
- **Cutoff wavelength λ_g (C3):** Using λ(nm) = 1240 / E_g(eV) → 1240 / 3.37 = **368 nm.** Note: the "absorption edge" experimentally is broader; Smijs & Pavel and other reviews quote ~370 nm; nano-ZnO can show absorption out to ≈ 380 nm because of band-tail states / size dispersion. So the *useful sunscreen edge* is reported variously as 370 nm (theoretical) up to 380 nm (broad-spectrum claim covers UVA1).

### 1.4 Refractive index (C1)

| λ | n (ordinary) | n (extraordinary) |
|---|---|---|
| 370 nm (just above gap) | ~2.10–2.30 | ~2.10–2.30 |
| 500 nm (visible) | 2.00–2.05 | 2.02–2.07 |
| 800 nm | ~1.95 | ~1.97 |

Standard reference value n ≈ 2.0 in the visible. (Bond, *J. Appl. Phys.* 36:1674 (1965); refractiveindex.info, Bond-o tabulation.) Lower n than TiO2 → less Mie scattering at any given size → less white-cast for the same particle size. This is the principal cosmetic advantage of ZnO over TiO2 at equal coverage.

### 1.5 Particle sizes used in sunscreen (C1)

| Grade | d50 typical | Use case |
|---|---|---|
| Pigment-grade ZnO | 200–300 nm | Older opaque sunscreens, calamine, zinc paste |
| "Microfine" / submicron | ~100–200 nm | Non-nano labels in EU; partial transparency |
| Nano-ZnO | 30–80 nm primary; agglomerates 100–200 nm | Modern transparent broad-spectrum sunscreens |

**Regulatory threshold (EU):** 50 % (number distribution) of particles ≤ 100 nm = "nano". (EU Cosmetic Reg. 1223/2009 Art. 2.1(k); refined definition in Recommendation 2022/C 229/01.) (C1)

SCCS/1489/12 (and 2024 revision) considers safe nano-ZnO **only** if D50 (volume) > 30 nm and D1 > 20 nm — i.e. very small particles (<20 nm) excluded. (C1)

Wakefield-style commercial dispersions (Croda Z-Cote, BASF, Symrise) commonly specify primary crystallite ~30–50 nm with stabilised aggregates 100–200 nm. (Patent US20060228310 specifies 70–130 nm volume-median.)

### 1.6 Surface coatings (C1 / C2)

ZnO is chemically reactive (slightly amphoteric, soluble in mild acid, and photocatalytic). Cosmetic-grade ZnO is virtually always coated. SCCS/1518/13 (revision 2014) lists coatings deemed safe at the time:

- **Triethoxycaprylylsilane** (the most common — C8 silane)
- **Dimethicone** (PDMS)
- **Dimethoxydiphenylsilanetriethoxycaprylylsilane crosspolymer**
- **Octyl triethoxy silane**

Subsequent SCCS opinions (SCCS/1625/20, SCCS/1654/23) extended approvals to silica and additional silanes.

**Function of coatings:**
1. **Suppress photocatalysis** (silica, alumina; see §3).
2. **Improve dispersion in oil/water phase** (silanes, dimethicone, stearate, isopropyl titanate).
3. **Reduce ionic Zn²⁺ release** (which can otherwise photo-degrade organic filters and cause skin irritation at high load).
4. **Improve sensory feel** (reduce drag).

Loading: typical surface treatments are 2–8 wt% relative to ZnO. (Croda technical bulletin "Z-Cote HP1"; BASF Z-Cote.)

## 2. UV Attenuation Spectrum

### 2.1 Mechanism (C1)

Inorganic UV filters are **wide-bandgap semiconductors**, *not* π→π\* chromophores. Two physical processes contribute to attenuation:

1. **Electronic absorption** above the bandgap — photons with E > E_g promote a valence-band electron to the conduction band. Below the gap, the crystal is essentially transparent.
2. **Mie scattering** — wavelength-comparable particles scatter light. Cross-section depends on size parameter x = πd/λ and refractive-index contrast (n_particle / n_medium).

Cole, Shyr & Ou-Yang (*Photodermatol. Photoimmunol. Photomed.* 32:5–10, 2016, doi:10.1111/phpp.12214) measured reflection of typical mineral sunscreen films across UV with an integrating sphere: **average reflection 4–5 %** through the UV — i.e. < SPF 2 from scattering alone. Conclusion: "Metal oxide sunscreens protect skin by absorption, not by reflection or scattering." Reflection rises to ~60 % above the bandgap (visible), which is why thick layers look white. (C1)

### 2.2 Cutoff (C1 / C3)

- Bandgap-derived sharp cutoff: 1240/3.37 ≈ **368 nm** (C3 calculation).
- Practical absorption edge in cosmetics: often quoted **370–380 nm** because of (a) particle-size distribution producing band-tail absorption, (b) Mie scattering at the long-UVA end. Smijs & Pavel 2011, Schneider & Lim 2019 both note "broad UVA-UVB absorption" with rolloff ~380 nm. (C2)
- This places ZnO **deeper into UVA1 than TiO2** — a key clinical advantage. UVA1 = 340–400 nm.

### 2.3 Particle size dependence (C1, Mie theory)

Popov, Priezzhev, Lademann, Myllylä (*J. Phys. D: Appl. Phys.* 38:2564–2570, 2005, doi:10.1088/0022-3727/38/15/006) used Mie theory + Monte Carlo simulation in stratum corneum:

- For 310 nm (UVB) light, **optimal TiO2 size = 62 nm** (extrapolation to ZnO with lower n shifts optimum slightly larger, ≈ 70–80 nm for UVB).
- For 400 nm (UVA), **optimal TiO2 size = 122 nm**.

Egerton & Tooley (*Int. J. Cosmet. Sci.* 34:117–122, 2012, doi:10.1111/j.1468-2494.2011.00689.x) compared 20, 50, 100 nm TiO2 — same physics applies to ZnO with shifted indices: smaller particles (~30 nm) give the highest *mass-specific* UVB attenuation but minimal UVA attenuation; ~100–150 nm particles give the highest UVA attenuation but visible whitening rises sharply because Mie scattering peak moves into the visible.

**Trade-off summary (C2):**

| ZnO particle size | UVB attenuation | UVA1 attenuation | Visible whitening |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20–30 nm | High (absorption-dominated) | Low | Low |
| 60–80 nm | High | Medium | Low–medium |
| 100–150 nm | Medium | High (Mie peak in UVA) | Medium |
| > 200 nm | Lower (mass) | High | High (white cast) |

### 2.4 Mass extinction coefficient (C2)

Unlike organic chromophores (where ε is intrinsic), the mass extinction coefficient α (L·g⁻¹·cm⁻¹) of ZnO depends on *particle size and dispersion quality*. Egerton 2012 reports for typical sunscreen-grade ZnO (~80 nm primary, 150 nm aggregate):

- α(310 nm) ≈ 6–10 L·g⁻¹·cm⁻¹ (UVB)
- α(360 nm) ≈ 4–7 L·g⁻¹·cm⁻¹ (UVA2)
- α(380 nm) ≈ 1–3 L·g⁻¹·cm⁻¹ (UVA1 edge)

(These are roughly half the equivalent figures for nano-TiO2 in the UVB; ZnO holds attenuation longer into UVA.) Caveat C4 — Egerton's figures are graph-derived from Mie computation; actual sunscreen formulas vary widely depending on dispersion.

### 2.5 ZnO vs TiO2 spectral comparison (C1)

- **TiO2 (rutile, ~20–50 nm)**: peak attenuation ~280–340 nm; sharply drops above 360 nm because rutile bandgap ≈ 3.0 eV and its higher refractive index means Mie scattering dominates the long-UVA, but mass-specific.
- **ZnO**: more uniform attenuation across UVB and UVA, extending to ~380 nm. Superior in UVA1 (340–400 nm).
- Hence "broadest spectrum" claim of ZnO. Combination ZnO + TiO2 is common for true broad-spectrum mineral sunscreens. Smijs & Pavel 2011 and Schneider & Lim 2019 both emphasise this complementarity.

## 3. Photocatalytic activity

### 3.1 Mechanism (C1)

**Critical distinction:** ZnO does *not* photodegrade in the way organic UV filters do. The crystal lattice is stable under UV. However, photogenerated electron–hole pairs (lifetime ~ns–μs) at the particle surface drive redox reactions in adjacent species, *catalysing* degradation of organic molecules and producing reactive oxygen species (ROS):

- **Conduction-band electron + O₂ → O₂•⁻ (superoxide)** (E°(O₂/O₂•⁻) = -0.33 V vs NHE; ZnO CB ~ -0.5 V → thermodynamically allowed).
- **Valence-band hole + H₂O / OH⁻ → •OH (hydroxyl radical)** (E°(•OH/H₂O) = +2.27 V; ZnO VB ~ +2.7 V → allowed).
- **Energy transfer to ³O₂ → ¹O₂ (singlet oxygen)** (less efficient than charge transfer for ZnO).

Wood, Scaiano et al. (*J. Phys. Chem. C* 120:2916–2926, 2016; doi:10.1021/acs.jpcc.5b11456) compared ZnO and ZnS — ZnO is more efficient at •OH and ¹O₂ generation; ZnS more efficient at O₂•⁻.

### 3.2 Quantitative ROS generation (C1 / C2)

- Lewicka, Hsiao, Yu, Colvin et al. (*J. Photochem. Photobiol. A* 263:24–33, 2013; doi:10.1016/j.jphotochem.2013.04.019) — "Photochemical behavior of nanoscale TiO2 and ZnO sunscreen ingredients". 8 commercial sunscreens compared. **ZnO derived from commercial sunscreens produced substantial ROS upon UVA illumination**, indicating coatings on ZnO products are *less* effective than on TiO2.
- Smijs & Pavel (*Nanotechnol. Sci. Appl.* 4:95–112, 2011): keratinocyte viability fell at ZnO ≥ 15 μg·mL⁻¹ in vitro; Zn²⁺ dissolution is part of the toxicity mechanism (a feature *unique* to ZnO vs TiO2).
- ZnO is **intermediate** between anatase TiO2 (most catalytic) and rutile TiO2 (least). Ranking (C2 consensus): anatase TiO2 > ZnO > rutile TiO2.

### 3.3 Effect of coatings (C1)

- **Silica coating** is the most effective. Sonochemically synthesised silica-coated ZnO showed >80 % reduction in methylene-blue degradation rate vs uncoated, but exact reduction depends on shell thickness. (Liufu et al., *Ultrason. Sonochem.* 18:893 (2011).) (C2)
- For TiO2, alumina/silica coatings have been reported to suppress photocatalysis by **>90 %** (Carlotti et al., *J. Photochem. Photobiol. B* 96:130 (2009); Egerton review 2012). (C2)
- ZnO coatings are generally **less effective than TiO2 coatings** because (a) ZnO partially dissolves at slightly acidic pH releasing Zn²⁺ and exposing fresh surface, (b) the coating chemistry is harder to anchor on ZnO than on TiO2.
- SCCS/1518/13 reaffirmed that triethoxycaprylylsilane, dimethicone, dimethoxydiphenylsilanetriethoxycaprylylsilane crosspolymer, and octyl triethoxy silane on nano-ZnO yield acceptable safety. SCCS does **not** specifically quantify ROS suppression; it considers downstream toxicology.

### 3.4 Bleaching of co-formulated organic filters (C1)

A direct, formulation-relevant consequence: uncoated/poorly-coated nano-ZnO and nano-TiO2 can accelerate degradation of avobenzone, octinoxate and other organic filters. Documented examples:

- Lewicka et al. 2013 measured ROS generation in commercial sunscreens — ZnO-containing products generated detectable ROS that correlated with degradation of dye markers.
- Tampucci, Burgalassi, Chetoni et al. (*Photochem. Photobiol. Sci.* 20:1593, 2021; doi:10.1007/s43630-021-00101-2) — "Zinc oxide-induced changes to sunscreen ingredient efficacy and toxicity under UV irradiation" — quantified loss of avobenzone and OMC in O/W emulsions co-formulated with ZnO.
- Avobenzone loss in TiO2/ZnO formulations can exceed 50 % after 2 MED of UV unless stabilisers (octocrylene, DEHN, polycrylene) are present, *and* the inorganics are well coated.

### 3.5 Implications for skin (C1 / C2 consensus)

- *In vitro* keratinocyte / fibroblast studies show ROS-mediated cytotoxicity at ZnO ≥ 10–30 μg·mL⁻¹ (uncoated, nano).
- *In vivo* coated nano-ZnO does not reach viable keratinocytes (see §4) — therefore intracellular ROS damage is unlikely under intact-skin use.
- SCCS, TGA, and FDA agree that **coated nano-ZnO at ≤ 25 % is safe on intact skin**; uncertainty remains for damaged skin and chronic / spray exposure.

## 4. Safety / Regulatory

### 4.1 Skin penetration (C1)

- Filipe, Silva, Steiner, Cardoso et al. *Skin Pharmacol. Physiol.* 22:266–275, 2009 (doi:10.1159/000235554) — nuclear microscopy of human skin biopsies after 2 h sunscreen application: ZnO and TiO2 detectable only on surface and outermost stratum corneum, even after 48 h occluded exposure. (C1)
- Cross, Innes, Roberts, Tsuzuki et al. *Skin Pharmacol. Physiol.* 20:148, 2007 — coated nano-ZnO (26–30 nm) penetration through human epidermis < 0.03 % over 24 h. (C1)
- Gulson, McCall, Korsch et al. *Toxicol. Sci.* 118:140–149, 2010 (doi:10.1093/toxsci/kfq243) — used isotope-labelled ⁶⁸ZnO sunscreen (19 nm) on humans for 5 days; detected trace ⁶⁸Zn in blood/urine — but at ~1/1000 of total Zn body load. The authors interpret this as *partial dissolution to ionic Zn²⁺*, not particulate penetration. (C1)
- Mavon, Miquel, Lejeune et al. *Skin Pharmacol. Physiol.* 20:10–20, 2007 — 20 nm TiO2 in decyl-glucoside vehicle: 83 % in stratum corneum (15 strippings), 5 % in viable epidermis, ~0.1 % in dermis (after exhaustive analytical methods). Largely consistent with "no significant penetration." (C1)

**Consensus:** intact stratum corneum is an effective barrier; both SCCS and TGA accept this for coated material at typical particle sizes. Damaged or flexed skin is an uncertain area.

### 4.2 Inhalation hazard (C1)

- IARC Monograph 93 (2010): **TiO2 = Group 2B** (possibly carcinogenic by inhalation, based on chronic rat studies). ZnO is **not** classified by IARC as a carcinogen.
- However, both ZnO and TiO2 nanoparticles can cause pulmonary inflammation by inhalation. SCCS/1489/12 explicitly states nano-ZnO **must not** be used in spray products — same restriction for nano-TiO2 (SCCS/1583/17).
- EU Annex VI entries 30/30a and 27/27a for non-spray formats only. (C1)

### 4.3 Coral / aquatic safety (C1 / C2)

- Hawaii Act 104 (effective 2021) bans **organic** filters oxybenzone & octinoxate (not ZnO/TiO2). Subsequent Act 15 (2021) bans avobenzone & octocrylene from 2023. ZnO is sometimes marketed as the "reef-safe" alternative — but this is partial:
- Corinaldesi, Marcellini, Nepote et al. *Sci. Total Environ.* 637:1279, 2018 (doi:10.1016/j.scitotenv.2018.05.108) — uncoated nano-ZnO induced rapid coral bleaching at 300 μg·L⁻¹; coated ZnO substantially less toxic.
- Tang, Liu, Wang et al. (*J. Hazard. Mater.* 2024) — transcriptomic effects on *Galaxea fascicularis* at sub-mg/L nZnO and nTiO2.
- Surface-coated, larger-particle ZnO is the preferred "reef safer" option but not zero-impact. The label "reef safe" has no FDA/EU regulatory definition.

### 4.4 Other regulatory observations

- **California Prop 65** lists "Titanium dioxide (airborne, unbound particles of respirable size)"; ZnO not listed.
- **OSHA / NIOSH** have inhalation REL/PEL for ZnO fume (0.5–10 mg·m⁻³), distinct from cosmetic use.
