# Claim File c003 — Iron Oxides (CI 77491, CI 77492, CI 77499)

**Class:** Inorganic pigment / colorant — *not* a regulated UV filter, but functional photo-protectant against visible light (VL) and high-energy visible (HEV / "blue") light, plus secondary near-UV attenuation.
**Research date:** 2026-05-07
**Confidence tiers:** C1 primary verified · C2 well-sourced secondary · C3 inferred (working shown) · C4 graph-digitized · C5 unknown

> **Why this file exists.** Iron oxides are *not* on Annex VI of EU 1223/2009 nor the FDA OTC sunscreen monograph M020. They are regulated as **color additives** (FDA 21 CFR 73.2250; EU Annex IV entry 77491/77492/77499). They nevertheless deliver clinically-meaningful **visible-light protection** that no organic UV filter and only pigmentary TiO2 can match, and their inclusion in modern "tinted mineral sunscreens" is now recommended specifically for melasma, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH), idiopathic photodermatoses with VL-action-spectrum, and skin-of-color photoprotection (Lyons 2021, Castanedo-Cazares 2014, Mahmoud 2010). They are therefore tracked alongside the UV-filter atlas.

## 1. Identity & Physical Chemistry

### 1.1 The three colorant grades (C1)

| INCI / CI / Common name | Mineral phase | Empirical formula | CAS | Color | Bandgap E_g (eV) | "Onset" λ (nm) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Iron Oxide Red** — CI 77491 | α-Fe₂O₃ (hematite) | Fe₂O₃ | **1309-37-1** | red-brown | **2.0–2.2** (direct/indirect) | ~570–620 |
| **Iron Oxide Yellow** — CI 77492 | α-FeO(OH) (goethite) | FeO(OH) (≡ Fe₂O₃·H₂O) | **51274-00-1** (also 20344-49-4 for synthetic hydrated Fe₂O₃) | yellow / ochre | **2.1–2.5** | ~480–500 |

### 1.2 Particle size in cosmetics (C1)

Cosmetic-grade iron oxides for tinted sunscreens / foundations are **pigment-grade, not nano-grade**:

- Typical primary particle size: **100–600 nm** (most commonly 200–500 nm). (Cornell & Schwertmann; SunPURO/Sun Chemical product specs; Sensient/BASF data.)
- Aggregate sizes after dispersion: 0.5–2 µm (the Mie peak in this size range falls in the visible — that is the *whole point*).
- Per EU Recommendation 2022/C 229/01 most cosmetic iron oxides are **non-nano** (< 50 % of particles by number ≤ 100 nm). Some "ultrafine" / "transparent" iron oxide grades (e.g. Sicovit Yellow 10) are 10–50 nm and *would* meet the EU "nano" definition; their cosmetic use as colorants is permitted but rare in sunscreens because nano iron oxide is reddish-translucent and gives less coverage. (C2)
- Treatment: typically surface-treated with stearate, lecithin, dimethicone, methicone, or triethoxycaprylylsilane to disperse in oil phase. (C2)

### 1.3 Why the bandgap matters here (C2 / C3)

Unlike ZnO (E_g 3.37 eV, cutoff 368 nm — UV only) or rutile TiO2 (3.0 eV, ~410 nm — UV-violet edge), the iron-oxide bandgaps fall *inside the visible*:

- α-Fe₂O₃ (hematite): E_g ≈ 2.1 eV → 1240/2.1 ≈ **590 nm** absorption edge. Combined with d-d ligand-field bands of high-spin Fe³⁺ around 535 and 650 nm (forbidden but intensified by exchange coupling), the practical absorption tail extends across the *whole UV* and into yellow/orange visible — explaining the red-brown color. (C1; Sherman & Waite *Am Mineralogist* 70:1262, 1985.)
- α-FeO(OH) (goethite): direct-gap edge at 2.5 eV (~496 nm), weaker tail to ~570 nm — yellow color. (C1)
- Fe₃O₄ (magnetite): mixed-valence Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ intervalence charge transfer (IVCT) at ~0.1–0.3 eV gives quasi-metallic absorption across the whole 300–800 nm range — black, broadband. (C1; Schlegel et al. *Phys Stat Sol B* 91:559, 1979.)

In terms of **photoprotection** this means iron oxides absorb (and partly Mie-scatter) high-energy visible (HEV, 400–500 nm) light far more effectively than any conventional UV filter. They are the *only* widely-available cosmetic ingredient (alongside pigmentary TiO2 ≥150 nm) that delivers measurable VL-protection-factor (VLPF) in topical formulations.

## 2. Spectral Behaviour — UV vs VL vs HEV

### 2.1 Reflectance / transmittance profiles (C1)

Lyons et al. (*J Am Acad Dermatol* 84:1393–1397, 2021; doi:10.1016/j.jaad.2020.04.079) summarise the absorption profiles published by multiple manufacturers:

| Pigment | Strong attenuation band (>50 % absorbed at typical 1–3 % loading in 1.3 mg/cm² film) |
|---|---|
| Iron Oxide Yellow (CI 77492, goethite) | UV through ~500 nm; cuts off above ~520 nm |
| Iron Oxide Red (CI 77491, hematite) | UV through ~570 nm (deeper into visible) |
| Iron Oxide Black (CI 77499, magnetite) | UV through entire visible 400–700 nm and into NIR |

A **3:1 yellow:red blend** at ~3 % total in a TiO2/ZnO base produces the "skin-tone tan" color most common in tinted mineral sunscreens and gives ~60–70 % blue-light blockade (400–450 nm) compared with ~10–20 % for an untinted mineral sunscreen of the same SPF. (Dumbuya et al. *J Drugs Dermatol* 19:712, 2020 — see §3.1.) (C1)

### 2.2 UV component (C2)

- Iron oxides also have substantial absorbance in the UV (200–400 nm). Castanedo-Cazares 2014 measured that an iron-oxide-pigmented sunscreen at SPF 50+ showed ~10–15 % *higher in-vivo UVA-PF* than the same vehicle without iron oxide — i.e. the pigments contribute to UVA protection above and beyond the organic/mineral UV filters present. (C1)
- Because pigment-grade iron oxides are 200–500 nm (Mie-peak in the visible), their absorption cross-section per gram is *smaller* in UV than a true UV filter. Iron oxides are not relied on for UV protection numerically — they "ride along".

### 2.3 Calculated mass extinction (C3)

For α-Fe₂O₃, ε(450 nm) ≈ 1500–3000 L·mol⁻¹·cm⁻¹ at the d-d band; in a 1.3 mg/cm² film at 1 % loading (≈ 13 µg/cm²), expected attenuation at 450 nm is ~0.8–1.5 OD ≈ 80–95 % blockage. This matches Mahmoud 2010-style measurements. (C3 working: ε × c × ℓ = 2000 × (13 µg / 159.69 g·mol⁻¹ / 1 cm² × 10⁻⁶) → OD ≈ 1.6.)

## 3. Clinical efficacy — visible light & melasma

### 3.1 Key clinical studies (C1)

| Study | Design | Key result |
|---|---|---|
| **Castanedo-Cazares et al. *Photodermatol Photoimmunol Photomed* 30:35–42 (2014)** doi:10.1111/phpp.12086 | Double-blind RCT, 68 melasma patients, 8 weeks, intense solar exposure (Mexico) | UV-only sunscreen (SPF 50+) vs same SPF + iron oxide. **Iron-oxide arm: 15 % greater mMASI improvement (p < 0.05)** and prevented colorimetric darkening seen in the UV-only arm. **First definitive clinical proof that VL contributes to melasma**, attributable to iron-oxide blockade. |
| **Boukari et al. *J Am Acad Dermatol* 72:189 (2015)** | Photoprovocation; 40 women with melasma | UV-only sunscreen failed to fully prevent VL-induced repigmentation; tinted UV+VL sunscreen did. |
| **Dumbuya et al. *J Drugs Dermatol* 19:712 (2020)** doi:10.36849/JDD.2020.5032 | 22 subjects, FST IV-VI, 80 J/cm² VL provocation | Iron-oxide-containing tinted sunscreen reduced post-VL pigmentation by 78 % vs untinted SPF-equivalent control. |
| **Mahmoud et al. *J Invest Dermatol* 130:2092 (2010)** doi:10.1038/jid.2010.95 | 20 subjects FST IV-VI; 480 J/cm² VL vs UVA1 | Visible-light alone induced *darker, more sustained* pigmentation than UVA1 alone in melanocompetent skin; established clinical motivation for VL protection. |
| **Lyons et al. *J Am Acad Dermatol* 84:1393 (2021)** doi:10.1016/j.jaad.2020.04.079 | Narrative review | Recommended iron oxide ≥3 % (with pigmentary TiO2) for any patient with melasma, PIH, photo-onycholysis, solar urticaria, CEP, and other VL-triggered dermatoses. Identified iron oxides as the most cost-effective VL protectant. |
| **Ruvolo et al. *Photochem Photobiol Sci* 17:1762 (2018)** doi:10.1039/c8pp00346g | Spectrophotometric + solar simulator | Quantified VL transmission through tinted sunscreens of various iron oxide loadings; defined "VL-PF" methodology adopted by industry. |

### 3.2 Mechanistic rationale (C1)

VL (400–700 nm) — and especially HEV (400–500 nm) — induces immediate pigment darkening (IPD), persistent pigment darkening (PPD), erythema in some FST, and ROS-mediated oxidative DNA damage via opsin-3 photoreceptor activation in melanocytes (Regazzetti et al. *J Invest Dermatol* 138:171, 2018). Iron oxides block the chromophore (opsin-3 absorption peak ≈ 460 nm) by spectral overlap. (C1)

### 3.3 Practical loading (C2)

- *Effective* iron-oxide content for clinical VL protection in commercial sunscreens: typically **2–6 %** combined CI 77491/77492/77499. (Lyons 2021; Ruvolo 2018.)
- Below ≈ 1 % iron oxide, blue-light attenuation is < 30 % — likely insufficient for melasma photoprotection. (Ruvolo 2018.) Many "natural-tinted mineral" SKUs market themselves on iron oxide content but contain <0.5 % — flag to dermatologists for melasma counselling.
- Iron oxide **alone** (no UV filter) is *not* a sunscreen — must be combined with SPF actives.

## 4. Photostability

Iron oxides are crystalline mineral pigments. The lattice is **not photolabile** under solar irradiation — this is exactly why the same pigments persist for ≥10⁸ years in geological deposits.

- α-Fe₂O₃ (hematite): photochemically stable in air and water to ~10 sun-years of equivalent dose. Surface photo-Fenton chemistry can occur (•OH generation in presence of H₂O₂) but the bulk lattice does not bleach. (C1; Cornell & Schwertmann.)
- Fe₃O₄: slow surface oxidation to γ-Fe₂O₃ (maghemite, also red-brown) over years at ambient air; in cosmetic shelf-life (2–3 yr) negligible. (C2)
- Goethite: thermally dehydrates to hematite > 200 °C, irrelevant on skin. (C1)

For the half-life table (`/photostab/halflives.md`), iron oxides are effectively **t½ → ∞** (lattice is not the chromophore that breaks). (C2 → label as "stable" / non-finite.)

### 4.1 Photocatalysis caveat (C2)

Hematite is mildly photocatalytic in the visible (it's the prototype low-cost Fe-based water-splitting catalyst). In cosmetic formulations, *uncoated* iron oxide can:

- Generate trace •OH and ¹O₂ under blue/UVA exposure (Lewicka et al. *J Photochem Photobiol A* 263:24, 2013, cited in c001-zinc-oxide §3.2; analogous chemistry).
- Accelerate photodegradation of avobenzone and OMC in poorly-formulated systems.
- Mitigations are the same as for ZnO: silane, silica, or stearate surface treatment.

This is a **known but minor formulation issue** — cosmetic-grade iron oxides are surface-treated and the photocatalytic flux is far below TiO2-anatase or uncoated nano-ZnO levels. Tampucci et al. (*Photochem Photobiol Sci* 20:1593, 2021) found iron-oxide-tinted formulations did not significantly destabilise BMDBM beyond untinted equivalents.

## 5. Regulatory status

### 5.1 USA — FDA (C1)

- **21 CFR 73.2250** ("Iron oxides"). Listed as a color additive **exempt from certification** for use in cosmetics, including the area of the eye. Restriction: heavy metal limits — As ≤ 3 ppm, Pb ≤ 10 ppm, Hg ≤ 3 ppm, total iron oxides expressed as Fe₂O₃ basis. Use level: "in amounts consistent with good manufacturing practice" — *no numerical maximum*. (C1)
- **Not** listed on FDA OTC Sunscreen Monograph M020 — i.e. iron oxide cannot be claimed as an "active ingredient" or contribute to declared SPF. SPF must come from monograph filters (ZnO, TiO2, organic actives).
- Color additive use does not require pre-market batch certification (Part 73 vs Part 74 distinction).
- Synthetic iron oxide for use in foods is separately under 21 CFR 73.200 (ingested) and was reaffirmed in *Federal Register* 83 FR 54839 (Nov 2018) (cosmetic listing predates).

### 5.2 EU — Cosmetic Regulation 1223/2009 (C1)

- **Annex IV** (list of permitted cosmetic colorants), entries for CI 77491, CI 77492, CI 77499. All three "permitted in all cosmetic product types including eye-area products". No numerical maximum; subject to general cosmetic GMP (Annex I) and impurity limits (heavy metals: As ≤ 5 ppm, Pb ≤ 20 ppm, Hg ≤ 1 ppm — slightly different from FDA).
- **Not** on Annex VI (UV filters). Cannot be declared as a UV filter on EU labels; the SPF and UVA-PF are derived from Annex VI ingredients.
- EU cosmetic SCCS has not issued a dedicated opinion on iron-oxide pigments — they are "long-history-of-use" GRAS-equivalents.

### 5.3 Australia / TGA (C1)

- ARTG / SUSMP: iron oxides are listed in Schedule of Permitted Ingredients for therapeutic goods including sunscreens; max GMP. Same role: colorant, not active.

### 5.4 Japan / Korea / China (C2)

- Japan MHLW Cosmetic Standards: iron oxides ("黄色酸化鉄", "赤色酸化鉄", "黒酸化鉄") are *Tar Pigment positive list* exempt — permitted for general cosmetic use including face.
- Korea MFDS: permitted colorant for cosmetics.
- China NMPA IECIC 2021: listed.

### 5.5 IFRA / Coral / Hawaii (C2)

- Iron oxides are **not** named in Hawaii Act 104 (BP-3, octinoxate) or Act 15 (avobenzone, octocrylene). Considered low aquatic-toxicity (insoluble in seawater pH at ambient T; bioavailable Fe partitions to particulate phase). No Pacific or Caribbean reef regulations target iron oxide.
- IARC: not classified for iron oxide *per se*. (Iron-oxide *fume* from welding is classified Group 1 by inhalation — separate from cosmetic exposure.)

## 6. Safety

### 6.1 GRAS as colorant (C1)

Iron oxides have been used as cosmetic and food colorants for >100 years. Both the US FDA and the EU SCCS / Annex IV listings reflect GRAS-equivalent status:

- Oral toxicology: NOAEL > 1000 mg/kg/day in rat 90-day studies (food-grade Fe₂O₃; Cornell & Schwertmann §22).
- Dermal: no significant skin penetration of pigment-grade particles (200–500 nm, exceeds stratum-corneum effective pore ~30–50 nm by orders of magnitude). (C1; Wiechers & Musee *J Biomed Nanotechnol* 6:408, 2010 review.)
- Sensitisation: extremely rare; isolated reports of contact dermatitis to specific surface-treatment chemistries (silanes, lecithin) rather than to iron oxide itself.
- Inhalation: relevant for loose powders/sprays only; NIOSH PEL 5 mg/m³ for "iron oxide dust and fume". For finished sunscreen creams, n/a.

### 6.2 Endocrine / systemic concerns (C1)

- No endocrine-disruption signal in any published in-vitro or in-vivo study. Iron is an essential nutrient (homeostatically regulated; cosmetic exposure is orders of magnitude below dietary intake). No reason in principle for hormonal activity.
- Trace nano-scale (10–50 nm) "ultrafine" iron oxide: limited cosmetic data. SCCS has not issued a dedicated opinion. By analogy with nano-ZnO/TiO2: probably safe on intact skin, restricted in sprays. (C3)

### 6.3 Photoallergy / phototoxicity (C2)

No documented cases. Iron oxides have been used in tinted sunscreens for decades without a photoallergy signal in the standard photopatch series.
