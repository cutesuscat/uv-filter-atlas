Structural relation to 4-MBC. 3-Benzylidene Camphor (3-BC) is the unsubstituted parent of 4-Methylbenzylidene Camphor (4-MBC, enzacamene). The molecules differ by one para-methyl group on the benzylidene phenyl ring. The chromophore — an α,β-unsaturated ketone formed by Knoevenagel condensation of camphor with benzaldehyde (or p-tolualdehyde for 4-MBC) — is electronically identical, so absorption maxima differ by only ~3 nm and photochemistry is qualitatively the same (E/Z photoisomerisation through twisted-S₁ conical intersection). Endocrine activity, by contrast, is shared — both molecules are estrogenic in standard yeast-ER and uterotrophic assays (Schlumpf 2001; Tinwell 2002). This convergent toxicology is why the EU banned 3-BC (2015) several years before 4-MBC (2024).

Bottom line. 3-BC is commercially obsolete in 2026. All major jurisdictions either ban it or have de-listed it. It is included in this atlas for chemical-historical completeness and because legacy EU formulations from before 2015 may still appear in older product testing.

Historic concentration in legacy EU products: typically 0.5 – 2 % (often paired with 4-MBC, OMC).

Section 2: UV Absorption Spectrum

UVB filter (280–320 nm) with a single strong band centred at ~298 nm. Spectrum is essentially identical to 4-MBC except for the small −CH₃ effect on the para position.

The chromophore is the C=C-C=O α,β-enone bridging the 3-position of camphor to the phenyl ring. The phenyl ring is the chromophoric extension; replacing the p-methyl (4-MBC) with H (3-BC) blue-shifts λmax by ~2–3 nm and reduces molar absorptivity by ~10 % (consistent with loss of weak +I donation).

Section 3: Photodegradation Kinetics

3-BC is photochemically essentially identical to 4-MBC: reversible E → Z isomerisation through a twisted-S₁ excited-state conical intersection, photostationary state with modest spectral red-shift, no significant fragmentation under solar doses.

  • Quantum yield of E→Z isomerisation: Φ ≈ 0.13 at 287 nm rising to ≈ 0.30 at 370 nm; wavelength-dependent, concentration- and oxygen-independent. (C1; Deflandre & Lang 1988; Buglak 2015 for the 4-MBC analogue.)
  • The photostationary state is itself photostable: after the initial dose, no further spectral change. Spectrum red-shifts ~5–8 nm at PSS but retains UVB absorption. (C1; Berset 1996.)
  • Quantum yield of irreversible photodegradation Φ_d ~ 10⁻³–10⁻⁴ (low). (C2; analogue 4-MBC data.)
  • Couteau et al. Pharm Res 24:1153 (2007) classified all benzylidene-camphor sunscreens as "photostable" with > 90 % retention at 25 MED in cosmetic emulsion. (C1)
  • Berset 1996 (Int J Cosmet Sci 18:167): under 5 MED simulated solar exposure, 3-BC family retained ~ 88 % initial absorbance — directly measured for the benzylidene-camphor scaffold. (C1)

Photoproducts (C2, by analogy)

  • Z-3-BC isomer (photostationary partner) — fully reversible; this is the dominant "product" under cosmetic exposure.
  • Trace retro-Knoevenagel fragmentation under intense UVC to benzaldehyde + camphor (negligible at solar doses). (C2; Stiehl 1992.)
  • No detectable singlet-oxygen sensitisation: triplet yield is low (Φ_T ≈ 0.05 by analogy with 4-MBC, Buglak 2015). Φ(¹O₂) ≈ 0.02. (C2)

Section 4: Notable Issues

  • Endocrine disruption — the reason for the EU ban (2015):
  • Schlumpf et al. (Environ Health Perspect 109:239, 2001) demonstrated estrogenic activity of 3-BC: positive in MCF-7 E-screen, positive uterotrophic assay (immature rat) at doses comparable to 4-MBC. (C1)
  • Schmutzler et al. (Endocrinology 148:2835, 2007) reported thyroid axis disturbance in rats (decreased serum T4, elevated TSH) for the camphor-sunscreen class as a whole, including 3-BC. (C1)
  • Mueller et al. (2003) and Schlecht et al. (Toxicol Sci 84:395, 2005) confirmed in vivo anti-androgenic effects in Hershberger assay. (C1)
  • SCCS opinion S61 / SCCS_Q_071 (June 2013) concluded MOS < 100 at 2 % use level, "use of 3-BC as a UV filter in cosmetic products at concentrations up to 2 % is not safe." (C1)
  • Outcome: Reg. (EU) 2015/1298, ban from August 2015. Listed Annex II entry 1379. (C1)
  • Skin penetration: lipophilic (log Kow ≈ 5); penetrates intact skin readily. Detected in human breast milk in the Schlumpf 2008 Chemosphere biomonitoring study. (C1)
  • ROS: low; not a primary safety concern.
  • Photoallergy: rare; isolated cases historically reported but commercial use was always small relative to 4-MBC, OMC, BP-3.
  • Aquatic toxicity: lipophilic, bioaccumulative. SCCS noted environmental concerns; not formally assessed by the EU water framework. Brausch & Rand 2011 review classified the camphor class as moderate ecological concern.
  • Synergy with avobenzone: none known; the benzylidene-camphor T₁ (~55 kcal/mol) is not low enough to quench the AVB diketo triplet (~60 kcal/mol) effectively. Reszko et al. (J Cosmet Dermatol 8:109, 2009) confirmed no useful TTET stabilisation. (C2)

Section 5: Why we still track this molecule

3-BC is now of historical interest only, but:

  1. Legacy product testing (e.g. archival cosmetic samples ≥ 2015) may contain it.
  2. Aquatic environmental monitoring still detects 3-BC in lake sediments and WWTP effluent decades after market withdrawal — its lipophilic, biopersistent profile means it remains an environmental tracer (Brausch & Rand 2011; Buser & Müller 2002 Environ Sci Technol 36:5097).
  3. It is the structural anchor for understanding the camphor-sunscreen class. The 4-MBC ban (2024) cited 3-BC's earlier ban as part of the "pattern of camphor estrogens" pharmacological argument (SCCS/1640/21).
