Key takeaways across the class

  1. Common photophysical mechanism — all five benzophenones rely on intramolecular hydrogen bonding (2-OH ↔ C=O) and ultrafast excited-state intramolecular proton transfer (ESIPT, k ≈ 10¹² s⁻¹) to dissipate UV energy non-radiatively. This gives the class moderate-to-good photostability and consistent dual UVB/UVA-II absorption profiles around 287 nm and 325 nm.
  2. Spectral limitation — none of the benzophenones in this collection extend meaningfully into UVA-I (>360 nm); they do not by themselves provide broad-spectrum (critical wavelength ≥370 nm) protection. They are typically combined with avobenzone, bemotrizinol, ecamsule, or zinc oxide for UVA-I coverage.
  3. Endocrine concern follows a structural ladder: BP-1 (the di-hydroxy metabolite) > BP-3 > BP-8 > BP-4 in in-vitro estrogenic potency. Topical BP-3 application produces measurable internal exposure to the more-potent BP-1 metabolite — this is the principal mechanistic basis for concern about sustained BP-3 use.
  4. Coral-reef toxicity has been demonstrated in primary literature only for BP-3 (Downs 2016 + Vuckovic 2022). Other benzophenones are inferred to be problematic by structural analogy but do not have comparable coral-planula datasets.
  5. Regulatory trajectory is clearly downward for the benzophenone class: - EU dropped BP-3 from 10% → 6% → 2.2% (body) over 2017–2022 - FDA classes all the listed benzophenones as "insufficient data for GRASE" - Hawaii, USVI, Palau, Aruba, Bonaire, Mexican marine parks have product-level bans naming BP-3 - Trolamine salicylate is proposed not GRASE outright
  6. Skin penetration data are striking but their clinical significance is unestablished — Matta et al. 2019/2020 in JAMA conclusively show BP-3 plasma concentrations exceed the 0.5 ng/mL FDA toxicology-trigger threshold at typical use; the FDA has been clear that exceeding the threshold does not equate to harm but does require additional safety data.
  7. Trolamine salicylate is in the file for completeness — it is salicylate-class (not benzophenone), is a weak UVB-only filter, and is essentially obsolete in sunscreens. It survives in OTC topical analgesics.
  8. Mexenone is essentially a museum specimen — historical Uvistat formulation only; no modern usage.

Summary recommendation hierarchy (by current weight of evidence)

For a formulator or consumer in May 2026, descending preference among this class:

  1. Sulisobenzone (BP-4) — water-soluble UVB+UVA-II filter; the lowest-concern member of the practical-use set; useful where lipid-soluble filters are unsuitable.
  2. Oxybenzone (BP-3) — still permitted in EU (2.2% body) and by FDA monograph (6%), but increasingly avoided due to coral-reef bans, photoallergy frequency, and endocrine concerns. Where regulation permits, it remains effective; where reef bans or sensitive-skin claims apply, alternatives are preferred.
  3. Dioxybenzone (BP-8) — limited modern usage; effectively replaced by better-characterized broad-spectrum filters.
  4. Mexenone (BP-10) — obsolete.
  5. Trolamine salicylate — not recommended as a sunscreen primary; FDA proposes "not GRASE."
  6. Benzophenone-1 (BP-1) — not approved as a UV filter in any major jurisdiction; relevant only as a metabolite/photoproduct of BP-3, not as a deliberate ingredient.
